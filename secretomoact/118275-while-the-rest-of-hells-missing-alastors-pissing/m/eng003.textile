Few dared to acknowledge him beyond stiff nods; his reputation was as much a presence as his shadow, bending faintly behind him.

He looked— On the outside—perfectly composed, just a gentleman taking an afternoon stroll to visit an old friend. On the inside? He was in absolute Hell. Not the Hell he always inhabited. A personal Hell. One that was quiet, mortifying, and utterly ridiculous to someone of his power. The one biological quirk that clung to him even after death, refusing to be shaken off the way his humanity had been.

And today it flared like a curse.

Rosie’s invitation still rested in his coat pocket; A neatly folded cream-colored card that smelled faintly of spiced peach blossoms. She had written in her flawlessly looping handwriting.

“Do stop by, dear Alastor! I’ve baked a fresh batch of my rose-petal scones, and I simply must hear what mischief you’ve been getting into lately.”

She meant well. She always meant well. Rosie’s cheerfulness was as dangerous as it was disarming, and she remained one of the very few who could coax a rare spark of genuine warmth from him.

But every visit to her parlor came with a trial.

Rosie’s teahouse was charming, bustling, and terribly public— And the facilities, while immaculate, were shared. Worse yet, the stalls were thin-walled, offering very little in the way of privacy. Every cough, every shuffle, every drip of a faucet echoed.

A nightmare.

Alastor felt it creeping up on him now, the pressure building steadily like an unwanted percussion section beneath his ribs. He kept walking, though a faint static ripple danced through the ends of his hair.

“Now, now,” he murmured aloud, his radio-voice crackling faintly. “No need for concern, my boy. A simple matter of timing. Perfectly manageable.”

His shadow, traitorous thing that it was, quivered skeptically.

Alastor watched her go.

Rosie’s voice faded into the clatter of the kitchen as she headed through the swinging doors, immediately scolding her staff with that particular brand of motherly severity only she possessed. Pots banged. Someone yelped. Fire whooshed. The entire building trembled with the sound of chaos.

Alastor rested his cane beside him, smoothing a gloved hand over the polished wood. His fingers were steady again. His coat settled perfectly against his shoulders. The constant prickle of static had faded to a soft, almost inaudible purr.

The worst of the panic had passed.

But the pressure had not.

He winced as that reminder pulsed, sharper now, as if his own body were making an impatient, indignant statement; ‘You cannot hold this forever.’

“Well,” he murmured, tapping one shoe against the floor. “It seems waiting is no longer a viable strategy.”

The truth was simple; The moment for trying earlier had been shattered by noise, panic, and interruptions. But now…?

He glanced around the parlor.

Only a handful of patrons remained, everyone else busy and focused in the kitchen. His mind didn’t feel spotlighted. Didn’t feel observed. And without the sensation of someone waiting, listening, worrying— He felt lighter. Less watched. Less judged.

His shoulders loosened. A small breath eased from his chest.

He stood. Took one step. Then another. His shadow followed, calm at last instead of writhing.

The hallway was still empty.

He reached the door of the restroom and paused. The last remnants of anxiety curled coldly at the edge of his ribs, but only faintly— Like an echo instead of a shout. He closed his eyes. Held the handle. Breathed.

“You can do this,” he whispered. Not a command. Not a threat. A reassurance.

He opened the door.

He didn’t rush. He slipped inside and locked it gently behind him. The soft lantern glow filled the room. It was quiet—blessedly, beautifully quiet. No banging. No shouting. No footsteps. Just peace.

The sort of peace he’d needed all along.

He exhaled slowly, tension dissolving with the breath.

When he moved to the stall this time, he didn’t tremble. He didn’t freeze. His mind didn’t scream with imaginary listeners. It was just him. And in that rare, fragile, perfect pocket of privacy and calm—

Alastor finally, finally found relief.

No struggle.

No panic.

Just a simple, natural release of tension that washed over him like warm sunlight breaking through storm clouds.

He braced one hand lightly against the stall wall and closed his eyes— Not in embarrassment, but in profound, exhausted gratitude.

The kind he would never admit aloud.

It was very, very noisy, and with no sounds masking it, Alastor was that much more aware of the racket he was making. To him, it sounded like a torrential rainstorm striking against a metal roof. Even when he tried aiming away from the water in the hope of quieting it down, each splash and hiss made his ears press more firmly against his head.

Still, he could not deny how truly nice it felt. His middle ceased its infernal cramping, the torment replaced by a loose fluttering as all of the muscles eased. He blew out a small sigh, as soft as possible. Certainly, no one could ever know how much he was enjoying this…

When it was over, he felt lighter, clearer and blessedly unburdened.

He straightened his coat, smoothed his gloves, and paused a moment before leaving, letting the remainder of his nerves settle like dust.

“…Thank goodness,” he muttered.

His reflection in the mirror looked calm. Present. The smile had evened out, genuine in its softness. He nodded to himself. Collected. Dignified again. And for once, that dignity hadn’t come from masking a flaw, but from overcoming it.

When he exited the restroom, the hallway was still empty. But the moment he stepped back into the parlor, Rosie burst through the kitchen doors, singed around the edges and holding a smoking wooden spoon like a defeated warrior.

“Everything is fine!” she announced brightly, then added in a whisper only he could hear, “The oven hates me.” She looked up—and froze.

Alastor stood tall, composed, and— Most importantly— Relaxed, with a soft, genuine smile.

Rosie gasped. “Oh!” she squeaked. “You… You look… Relieved!”

He chuckled, a warm, quiet sound. “Indeed, my dear. Your… Efforts were not in vain.”

Her face bloomed with pride and affection, radiant as her namesake flower. “Oh, sweetheart,” she breathed softly, “I’m so glad.”

He inclined his head. “As am I.”

Rosie stepped forwards and gently straightened a rumpled corner of his coat. “Feeling better?” she asked.

He hesitated— Only a moment. Then nodded. “Yes,” he said. “Very refreshed.”

And Rosie smiled like that was the best news Hell had offered all year.

Rosie set the charred wooden spoon aside with an exasperated sigh before ushering Alastor back to their little table, as though shepherding a guest of honor to a seat of prestige— Even though he looked perfectly capable of seating himself.

But the way she hovered beside him, smoothing nonexistent wrinkles from the tablecloth, adjusting the teacup angles by a degree or two, and brushing dust off the cushion that wasn’t there…

She wasn’t fussing over the table.

She was fussing over HIM.

And Alastor, to his own astonishment, didn’t bristle at it.

He settled into his seat with a soft, tired hum. His posture was straight, poised, no longer tense. His fingers steepled lightly atop the table, movements fluid rather than stiff.

Rosie beamed. The building could be on fire, the oven could be plotting rebellion, her staff could be in tears— But, Alastor looking calm? Oh, that was a victory.

She took the seat beside him again— Closer than before, but still respectful of his space— And poured fresh tea. “Here,” she said gently. “For your nerves.”

He arched a brow. “My dear, I assure you— My nerves are quite restored.”

“Well,” she replied, pouring anyway, “Then this is for celebration.”

He let out a soft chuckle. “Celebration, hm? For using the restroom unimpeded? How far I’ve fallen.”

“Oh hush,” Rosie said, lightly swatting his arm. “It was as important as anything else.”

His smile softened. “If you say so.”

“I do.” She glanced at him sidelong as she set the teapot down, studying the lines of his face— The more subtle ones, hidden beneath the usual theatricality. The softened brow. The relaxed jaw. The absence of forced cheer or tightly coiled control. He looked… At ease.

She hadn’t realized how badly she’d wanted to see him like that.

“Feels better now, doesn’t it?” she asked, voice dipping warm and quiet.

Alastor hesitated. Then said honestly, “Yes. Quite.”

Rosie’s shoulders relaxed. “Good. I know it was difficult.”

He gave her a look; Wry, but not sharp. “My dear, ‘difficult’ is rather an understatement.”

She bit her lip, amused and sympathetic. “Then what would you call it?”

He hummed, tapping his cane idly against his chair. “A prolonged exercise in suffering. An unending gauntlet. A trial of will and sanity.”

She laughed. “So dramatic.”

“I have a reputation to uphold.”

“Well,” she said with a prim little sniff, “You upheld it. Even if you were squirming the whole time.”

He nearly choked on his tea. “Rosie!”

“What?” She blinked innocently. “You were.”

“Do not use that word.”

“Squirming?”

“Rosie—!”

She giggled, absolutely delighted. “Fine, fine! I’ll stop.”

He huffed. “You are insufferable.”

“And you adore me,” she said with breezy confidence.

He opened his mouth— Paused— Felt heat rise to his ears— And promptly looked away.

Rosie’s giggle softened to a fond hum.

Silence fell between them— Not awkward or heavy, but warm. Gentle. The kind that existed only between people who trusted each other far more than they ever admitted aloud.

After a moment, Rosie folded her hands in her lap and spoke carefully. “Alastor… I want you to know something.”

He turned, curious.

She met his gaze squarely. She wasn’t stern, but sincere. Soft. “You can always tell me when you’re uncomfortable,” she said. “Always. I won’t judge. I won’t tease— At least not about that.” She winked.

Alastor stared at her for a long moment. His expression didn’t shift dramatically. No cracks. No flickers. No theatrical flourish. Just a slow, quiet exhale as something unspoken eased deep in his chest. “…Thank you, Rosie,” he said softly.

“You’re welcome,” she said, equally gentle.

He looked into his tea, watching the surface ripple faintly. “I am not accustomed to such… Compassionate intrusions.”

Rosie smiled knowingly. “You are now.”

He gave her a sidelong look, amused. “You presume too much.”

“No,” she said. “I just know you.”

And the room felt warmer for it.

A staff member peeked out from the kitchen, face sooty, hair frazzled. “Ms Rosie? The oven… Stopped making sparks.”

“Good!” Rosie called cheerfully. “Be grateful.”

The staff member paled and retreated at once.

Alastor snorted into his tea. “Your employees are terrified of you.”

“They’re terrified of you too,” she countered.

“Yes,” he said smugly, “but I intend it.”

Rosie rolled her eyes. “They’re lucky you were too busy panicking to notice their mistakes in there.”

He stiffened. “I was not—”

She gave him a look.

He sagged. “…Fine. Perhaps a touch.”

Rosie giggled and nudged his shoulder gently—Just a brief touch, but meaningful.

Alastor didn’t pull away. Instead, he allowed a tiny, genuine smile to curl across his lips. The worst was behind him. The humiliation had faded. And Rosie… Rosie was still beside him.

That, more than anything, grounded him.

He lifted his teacup in a small toast. “To… an unexpectedly eventful visit.”

Rosie tapped her cup to his. “To getting through it.”

They drank.

And for the first time all day, Alastor felt wholly, beautifully steady.
